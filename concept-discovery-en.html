Observation methods for discovering concepts from a subject

Purpose: To discover the contours of discussable Concepts from the states, differences, relationships, effects, and boundaries that appear in a subject, rather than accepting the subject through existing names or classifications.

This book does not prescribe a reading order or investigation procedure for any particular subject. Rather than defining a Concept in advance and fitting the subject into it, this book organizes viewpoints for tracing a contour from multiple observations and discovering what that contour represents.

A Concept is not certified by counting how many conditions it satisfies.

The world keeps moving, and the observer is not fixed. A Concept does not preserve the state of the world itself, nor is its correctness guaranteed at the moment it is discovered.

At the same time, we do not draw an arbitrary line and call it a Concept. When observable differences, repetitions, boundaries, effects, and evaluations persist as matters for discussion, we treat the contour they form as a candidate.

Depending on where the subject is observed from, the same thing can appear as a part or as a whole, and the same transition can appear as continuity or as a break.

For that reason, this book does not place one absolute contour first.

failure
state
lifecycle
identity
dependency
composition
transition
authority
scope
observation
criterion

Cut the subject along multiple axes and overlay the boundaries that appear each time.

The responsibilities, states, relationships, prohibitions, transitions, and criteria that recur across observations become candidates for a Concept.

Concept Discovery is not the work of assigning names. It is the work of bringing back a contour that can be discussed from a world that never stops moving.

Part I — Discovering the Contour

1. Trace the Contour Before Defining the Concept

Do not begin an investigation by trying to place the subject into a known classification.

What appears first is a set of fragmentary findings.

state is held here
retry is stopped here
authority is checked here
the path branches here

Each one may be a fact, but a fact by itself is not yet a Concept.

A contour begins to appear when multiple findings point, from different places, to the same responsibility or boundary.

finding
finding
finding
   ↓
point to the same boundary
   ↓
contour of a Concept

The important thing is not to decide the name first.

Use names to make the contour easier to see. Do not force the contour to fit the name.

Do not merely collect fragments. Look for the contour that repeatedly appears behind them.

2. Fix the Observation Position

The same subject has different contours depending on where it is observed from.

For example, a Task may appear as:

from the human          one piece of work
from the Loop           a segment up to the Goal
from the engine         a state unit that transitions
from persistence        something to restore

There is no need to choose one of these as the single correct Task.

First decide which relationship, responsibility, or state transition you are observing from.

Once the observation position is fixed, the contour visible from that position can be discussed.

Place it beside a contour taken from another position, and the mismatch itself becomes a design question.

the human sees one thing
the engine divides it into two

That difference is not merely a difference in explanation. It may show that the boundaries of responsibility or state do not coincide.

Before speaking about the contour of a Concept, fix the position from which that contour is being observed.

3. Read Responsibilities, Not Feature Names

A subject contains many product-specific names.

Learning names such as workflow, facet, reviewer, or task does not by itself make the knowledge reusable in another design.

Look at what the feature is responsible for.

what it holds
who reads it
when it is created
where it is stored
what it passes onward
what changes its state

If the same responsibility appears in another feature, that responsibility may be a higher-level Concept than the product-specific feature.

Conversely, if the responsibilities differ even when the names are the same, there is little reason to treat them as the same Concept.

Read the subject not as a collection of names, but as an arrangement of responsibilities.

4. Look at the Moment Something Is Lost, Not Only the Happy Path

On a successful path, many states can remain implicit and the system can still work.

Boundaries become exposed when something fails.

retry
resume
requeue
timeout
abort
conflict
stale
missing
exceeded

Reading paths like these reveals:

  • what must be identified again
  • what must be the source of truth
  • what must not be executed twice
  • what must be preserved as the last normal state

Failure does not only break a Concept.

It makes normally hidden responsibilities and boundaries observable.

A recovery path often expresses the design philosophy more clearly than a successful path.

5. Look at States the Design Refuses to Let Exist

A design appears not only in what it executes, but also in the states it refuses to allow.

do not execute twice
do not let a stale actor write
do not make an unverified state authoritative
do not act outside authority
do not advance with an incomplete result

Implementations use tools such as:

guard
validation
version
lease
fence
assert
reject

There is no need to generalize the tools themselves.

If multiple places protect against the same prohibited state, there is an invariant behind that prohibition.

A failure path shows what becomes visible after something breaks. An invariant shows what the design refuses to let become a broken state in the first place.

Do not mine Concepts only from existing features. Mine them from the states the design refuses to allow.

6. Separate What Can Be Seen from What Cannot

Not every property of a Concept can be observed from the same place.

active in the UI
terminated at runtime

successful from the outside
durable state still unsettled inside

present in the log
absent from authoritative state

The question is not merely how much observability exists.

Ask which property is visible from which observation surface, and what misconception is caused when that property is missing from view.

For example, completion may not be established from a UI display alone.

Having authority may not be established from a configuration value alone.

If a Concept is defined only by what can be observed, invisible parts are easily mistaken for nonexistent parts.

What can be perceived, and what cannot, is also part of a Concept's contour.

7. Do Not Collapse Every Kind of “Absent” into One

When something cannot be found, do not immediately conclude that it does not exist.

For example, there is no checkpoint can describe several different states.

it does not exist at all
it exists but is not visible from this API
it exists but the current actor cannot read it
it exists but has not yet been materialized
it existed but has expired
there is no means to observe it

The same problem occurs with state, authority, history, and evidence.

nonexistent
unknown
inaccessible
hidden
expired
not yet created

Collapsing these into one kind of absence distorts the boundary of a Concept.

Even when observing absence, first fix the observation position and what can be observed from it.

Do not turn “not visible” and “does not exist” into the same finding.

Part II — Following State and Time

8. Follow State, Not Files

Reading one file from beginning to end does not necessarily reveal the design responsibility.

State moves through multiple places.

schema
  ↓
loader / builder
  ↓
runtime
  ↓
persistence
  ↓
resume / failure
  ↓
test

If one Task state is defined in a schema, updated at runtime, stored in another file, and reused on resume, read that sequence as one path.

Following the same state reveals one responsibility across implementation files.

A Concept appears not at file boundaries, but along the path through which state moves.

9. Read the Lifecycle Along the Time Axis

Look not only at where state exists, but also at the states it passes through over time.

create
  ↓
active
  ↓
suspend
  ↓
resume
  ↓
complete
  ↓
retire

These are not separate Concepts placed side by side.

They trace the contour that one Concept has along the time axis.

For each transition, look at:

  • when it begins to exist
  • when it gains meaning or authority
  • what invalidates it
  • what is retained while suspended
  • what remains after it ends

Following transitions as one line through time exposes boundaries that cannot be seen from a state at a single moment.

The contour of a Concept appears not only in values at one moment, but also in transitions along the time axis.

10. Look for the Core That Survives Transition

The same Concept can change many things over time.

For a Task, the hypothesis, implementation, owner, work method, or verification method may all change along the way.

Even so, it may continue to be treated as “the same Task.”

start
  ↓
investigate
  ↓
implement
  ↓
fail
  ↓
redefine
  ↓
resume

The point is not to search for an immutable component.

Look at what may change while the thing is still treated as the same, and what change causes it to be treated as a different thing.

In implementation, identity may be supported by things such as:

identity
operation id
generation
version
owner
parent
checkpoint

But the same identifier does not necessarily mean the same Concept.

Conversely, even when components are replaced, the design may continue to track the thing as the same one.

There is no need to solve the Ship of Theseus.

Look at what the design itself adopts as the rule of identity.

Find the core that survives transition, and read what makes the design treat something as the same thing.

11. Separate Reversible from Irreversible Change

Transitions along the time axis include both reversible and irreversible ones.

draft → approved
open → closed
candidate → canonical
temporary → committed

Even if a previous state can apparently be restored, the world as a whole may not return to the same state.

the file can be restored
the fact that an external API was called cannot

state can be restored
information already seen by a human cannot

a setting can be restored
the history of an approval that once took effect remains

Read these separately:

reversible state change
compensation
reconstruction
rollback
irreversible effect

A rollback is not a snapshot of the state of the world itself.

Looking at which boundary, once crossed, prevents the situation from being the same as before reveals changes that matter to the Concept.

Do not read only reversibility. Read the weight of a transition from what cannot be restored.

12. Separate Working Context from Durable State

The context an Agent is reading now and the state preserved for later resumption serve different purposes.

working context
  used for the current judgment

durable state
  preserved across sessions

event log
  records what happened

artifact
  remains as the result of work

If these are treated as one thing, resumption may require rereading a large history, or the reasons needed for a later judgment may be lost.

In implementations with resume or retry, look at which state is reused.

What is carried from working context into durable state is not merely a question of storage volume.

It is a judgment about what must still be treated as part of the Concept at a later point in time.

Separate information needed now from information needed later for reconstruction.

13. Read Reference Status, Not “The Real One”

The same Concept may appear through multiple states or representations.

state → UI
state → cache
state → report
state → index

All of them may represent the Concept.

What matters is the asymmetry that appears when the representations disagree.

which one is used as the basis for updates
which one is used to reconstruct the others
which one can be lost and rebuilt
which one takes precedence in a conflict

canonical does not mean the Concept itself or an absolute truth.

It only means that, within the design, something has a role that is treated as a reference.

Do not ask which one is real. Read which one is treated as the reference.

14. Separate Where State Lives from How It Moves

The place where information exists and the mechanism that carries it onward are different things.

where it lives
  persistent environment
  Task-specific layer

how it moves
  Loop handoff path

For example, Task-specific knowledge or policy may exist, but if it is not passed to the next cycle or another owner, the handoff path is not established.

Conversely, even if a handoff mechanism exists, if the source of truth to be carried is not defined, the state is reinterpreted every time.

Separating storage location from transport lets the responsibility for holding a Concept and the responsibility for moving it be observed independently.

Do not read what is held and how it is carried as the same mechanism.

Part III — Reading Boundaries from Relationships

15. Read the Conditions Under Which a Concept Holds from Its Dependencies

A Concept does not necessarily stand on its own.

B holds because A exists
C is valid only while B exists
D loses its meaning when C is lost

The point is not to make a dependency inventory.

Look at what can be removed before the Concept no longer holds as that Concept.

For example:

approval
  depends on
request identity + current state + authority

resume
  depends on
checkpoint + environment + assumptions

Then ask which dependencies are replaceable and which ones, if lost, collapse the meaning itself.

If the time axis shows when something continues as the same thing, the dependency axis shows what supports it in continuing to hold as the same thing.

Strip away external conditions and trace the contour of the Concept from the point where its meaning collapses.

16. Read Part and Whole from Composition

Dependency and composition are not the same.

whole
 ├─ A
 ├─ B
 └─ C

A, B, and C may constitute one whole even if they do not depend on one another.

Look at:

  • what is included inside it
  • which parts are replaceable
  • which parts, if removed, make the whole cease to hold
  • whether it can still be called the same whole after one part changes

The important point is not to treat part and whole as absolute sizes.

The same subject can be a whole from one observation position and a part of a larger Concept from another.

A Task is the whole of a piece of work
A Task is a part of a Loop

Both can hold.

Part and whole change with the observation position. Composition asks what constitutes one thing from that position.

17. Read Authority Through Actor, Target, and Change

Responsibility and the power to make a change take effect are not the same.

ownership
  who is responsible

authority
  who can cause what to happen to what

For example, an actor may be able to create a request while only another actor can make an approval take effect.

An actor may be able to read state while only a limited actor can update the state used as a reference.

Listing actors alone does not reveal authority.

Read the relationship:

who
can cause what change
to what

Trace the contour of authority from the asymmetry among the actor, the target, and the change that can be made to take effect.

18. Read Scope from the Edge of Effect

Composition asks what is included inside something. Scope asks how far an effect or rule remains meaningful.

valid only for this Task
valid only during this Loop
does not cross this session
applies in this repository but does not reach an external system

This is not merely a containment boundary.

Look at where these change:

for whom
for what
under what conditions
how far it applies

This is not limited to effects that actually occur. Even a hypothetical rule has an observable scope if the conditions and edge of its applicability can be discussed.

Do not ask only what is contained. Look at where the effect of an action or rule ends.

19. Look Between One Concept and Another

A Concept is not necessarily self-contained.

An independent responsibility can appear in what passes between two Concepts.

Maker
  ↓
artifact / diff / evidence
  ↓
Checker

Or:

working context
  ↓
selection / compression
  ↓
durable state

Crossing such a boundary carries responsibilities such as:

  • what is passed
  • what is not passed
  • which form it is converted into
  • what must be true to cross the boundary
  • who holds authority next

This is neither dependency nor composition.

Look for a Concept in the handoff, conversion, promotion, or rejection that arises between A and B.

Look for contours not only inside Concepts, but also between one Concept and another.

20. Separate Evaluation from State Change

Obtaining a result and moving to the next state because of that result are different things.

Observation
   ↓
Judgment
   ↓
State word
   ↓
Transition

An implementation may collapse these four into one function or one output.

When you see REJECT, for example, look not only at the fact of rejection but also at where these are held:

reason
retry condition
next route

Judgment classifies the Concept. Transition changes the world.

Separating them reveals the boundary between observation and action.

Do not treat what was judged and what was caused to happen as the same responsibility.

21. Read the Boundary Between Inference and Structure

Separate the part where an Agent interprets meaning from the part fixed by an engine or other mechanism.

Inference
  judges meaning
  creates candidates
  interprets the situation

Structure
  holds state
  selects input
  applies transitions
  enforces Bound
  preserves durable state

If every important boundary is left to the Agent's memory or judgment, the design leans toward inference.

If everything is fixed in structure, context-dependent judgment is lost as well.

The point is not to decide which is superior.

Look at what remains as inference, what has been moved into structure, and what responsibility is placed on that boundary.

Read the design philosophy from what was moved out of inference into structure and what was left to inference.

22. Read Boundaries from What Changes Together and What Splits Apart

A Concept also appears in the propagation of change.

If a feature addition or fix repeatedly changes these together:

schema
runtime
persistence
resume
test
docs

then they may share one responsibility.

At the same time, place similar subjects side by side and look at the points where their treatment diverges.

A is retained
B is discarded

A is resumed
B is regenerated

A is established by a human
B is established by the engine

Even when the Concept itself cannot be stated directly in words, it is possible to observe where the same treatment is applied and where it diverges.

co-change
  candidate for the same responsibility

divergence
  candidate for a different contour

Read Concept boundaries from both the places that change together and the places that look similar but split apart.

Part IV — Retaining It as a Concept

23. Separate “It Holds” from “It Is Good”

For something to hold and for it to be judged good are different things.

A works
B also works

but A is chosen

The difference may be shaped not only by explicit rules, but also by criteria such as readability, symmetry, simplicity, convention, or experience.

Some of it may only be describable as taste.

Even then, if the observations behind the judgment can be shown, the evaluation criterion itself can be read as part of the Concept's contour.

This is not permission for arbitrariness.

If there is no observed difference or relationship and the line was drawn at random, it is not Discovery.

A criterion does not guarantee correctness in advance, but it can be observed, discussed, broken, and updated.

Observe not only what holds, but also what is treated as good.

24. Docs Are Maps; Implementation and Tests Are Primary Sources

Docs are useful for learning terminology and finding entry points.

But the design described in docs is not necessarily the same as the implementation that actually maintains and transitions state.

When checking the contour of a Concept, prefer roughly this order:

test / runtime artifact
        ↓
implementation
        ↓
schema / configuration
        ↓
official docs
        ↓
Issue / discussion

Use Issues less as sources that define a Concept and more as sources for seeing what happens when a boundary breaks.

The value of a source changes with the observation position, but when claiming that a Concept holds, prefer boundaries that are actually enforced.

Prefer the boundary that is actually enforced over the design that is merely described.

25. Remove Product-Specific Names and See What Remains

Remove product-specific terms from the findings you extracted.

facet
workflow
companion
repertoire

Replace them with general terms.

If you can still explain:

what is held
what is carried
what is evaluated
what is prevented
what it depends on
what is tracked as the same thing

then it may be worth preserving as a general Concept.

If the meaning disappears as soon as the product-specific name is removed, it is closer to a feature description.

But do not erase the contour merely to generalize it.

What remains after the name is gone is not an abstract word. It is responsibility and boundary.

A Concept keeps its contour even after it loses its name.

26. Do Not Force the Subject into an Existing Model

A classification you already have is a hypothesis for reading the subject, not the conclusion.

For example:

persistent environment
Task-specific layer
Loop handoff path

Even if these three explain much of the subject, there is no need to force everything into them.

If contours like these appear repeatedly, treat them as candidates for a new Concept.

has its own state
has its own lifecycle
has its own dependency
has its own authority or scope
the same boundary appears from another observation position
remains independently after failure
cannot be derived simply from another Concept

This is not a scorecard.

These are clues for seeing whether multiple observation axes point to the same contour.

If you begin adding exceptions merely to preserve the classification, question the classification instead.

Do not fit the subject to the model. Break the model with the subject.

27. Stop When Additional Reading No Longer Changes the Judgment

There is no need to read the entire subject.

The Goal of Concept Discovery is not completeness. It is to reach a point where a judgment can be made about the contour.

When reading more implementation or tests about the same responsibility no longer changes:

  • the classification
  • the state boundary
  • the understanding of the lifecycle
  • the understanding of dependency
  • the understanding of authority or scope
  • the understanding of the criterion
  • the understanding of failure
  • the set of responsibilities that appear

then the value of further exploration decreases.

Conversely, if one failure path, one dependency, or one irreversible transition breaks the existing model, that place is worth reading more deeply.

Stop not because “enough material has been read,” but because additional evidence no longer changes the contour.

Completion is not measured by file count, but by whether additional evidence changes the contour of the Concept.
